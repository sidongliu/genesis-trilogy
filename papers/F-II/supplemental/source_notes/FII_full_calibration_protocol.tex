\section{Agreement, calibration and fault transfer}\label{sec:model}

The finite model below tests common correctness and fault propagation.
It does not reconstruct a Type~III algebra or geometric response.
A bank of \(4096\) binary variables evolves under a hidden public
event \(c_k=\pm1\), which flips with probability \(0.01\) per epoch.
Each variable is retained with probability \(0.65\), replaced by a fair
coin with probability \(0.25\), or drawn with mean \(0.6c_k\) with
probability \(0.10\). Two drifting binary instruments have response
mean \(g_i z+b_i\), with gain near \(0.55\) and both common and
private bias components. Each observer has \(512\) reads per epoch.
Reference reads use known \(+1\) and \(-1\) inputs through that same
instrument; they displace world reads. Agreement coupling exchanges
the two current estimates and adjusts their biases. Weighted coupling
also uses the partner's reported gain. The scorer alone sees \(c_k\).
Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} specifies the full protocol.

For binary reports define \(A=P(\hat c_1=\hat c_2)\),
\(C_i=P(\hat c_i=c)\), and
\(E=P(\hat c_1=\hat c_2\ne c)\). Eventwise counting gives
\[
 C_1+C_2=1+A-2E,\qquad
 P(\text{correct}\mid\text{agree})=1-E/A\quad(A>0).
\]
These identities impose no independence or equal-accuracy assumption.

Agreement coupling increases agreement from \(.647\) to \(.837\)
while the reliability of an agreeing pair falls from \(.896\) to
\(.851\). Common error nearly doubles. With a common bias component
of zero, nominal or twice nominal amplitude, agreement-only common
error is respectively \(.041,.125,.275\); \(64\) reference reads per
sign hold it near \(.019,.020,.022\). A known input can expose a
shared bias that agreement leaves unconstrained. Among the tested
reference budgets \(4,16,32,64,128\), \(64\) has the highest
correctness. This is a tested optimum, not a proof of a global
optimum: spending \(128\) reads per sign leaves fewer world reads.

A \(100\)-epoch fault beginning at a random epoch either adds a clipped
bias of nominal size \(0.30\), or sets one instrument's gain to zero.
The loss of correctness of the undamaged observer, relative to a
matched no-fault run, shows the second role of coupling.
With \(16\) references per sign and unweighted agreement, a zero-gain
fault at observers \(2\) and \(1\) transfers losses \(.121,.123\).
Weighting by the partner's reported gain reduces them to \(.014,.016\);
with \(64\) references the weighted losses are \(.003,.004\).
A calibrated one-way leader failing at zero gain costs its follower
\(.389\), whereas the follower's failure cannot reach the leader.
No-coupling controls have zero transfer by construction.
The standard errors of these losses are at most \(.014\).

The reference that removes a bias does not recover information from a
zero-gain reading. Weighting helps because its additional gain report
addresses that failure. The undamaged observer loses roughly \(.32\)
or \(.29\) if only the damaged partner has reference access, compared
with \(.01\) or \(.02\) if only the undamaged observer has it.
Location of paid references matters as well as their number.

A diagnostic isolation run suppresses the coupling during the known
fault window. At \(16\) references the zero-gain loss decomposes as
\(.009\) of foregone ordinary coupling benefit and \(.112\) of harm
from following the faulty partner. Isolation uses the scorer's fault
time; it is not a deployable detection policy or an unavoidable risk
floor. A separately written implementation checks selected cases and
finds coupling harm \(.110\) with equal weights and \(.0036\) with
weights, and total losses \(.119,.013\). The simulation supports fault
transfer and its reduction in this protocol; it supplies no universal
consensus-to-geometric-stability law.

Communication uses \(11\) bits per scalar: \(22\) per epoch for
agreement, \(44\) for weighted agreement, \(11\) for a one-way leader,
and none for references alone. All observers start with \(4096\)
reference reads. Rules are designed, the world is uncontrolled, and
only two readers and these two fault types are tested. Raw simulation
output and the full legal clipping, update order and seeds remain
part of the accompanying evidence.


\section{Simulation protocol for Section~\ref{sec:model}}\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{World.} The bank has 4096 binary variables, which evolve independently given the public event. With $d_k=0.6\,c_k$, a variable that was $+1$ becomes $+1$ with probability $(1+0.65+0.10\,d_k)/2$ and one that was $-1$ becomes $+1$ with probability $(1-0.65+0.10\,d_k)/2$; the count of $+1$ variables is updated exactly from two binomial draws. The event $c_k$ flips with probability $0.01$ per epoch. No control acts on the world.

\paragraph{Instruments.} Observer $i\in\{1,2\}$ has gain $g_i(k)=0.55+0.17\sin(2\pi k/420+\phi_i)+0.03\sin(2\pi k/67+\phi_i)$ and bias $b_i(k)=s\,(0.10\sin(2\pi k/250)+0.03\sin(2\pi k/53))+0.10\sin(2\pi k/300+\psi_i)+0.025\sin(2\pi k/43+\psi_i)$, with $\phi=(0,1.7)$, $\psi=(0.4,2.9)$ and common-contamination scale $s=1$ unless stated. Every instrument is clipped to the legal region $|b|\le0.98-|g|$. A read of a variable $z_j$ returns $+1$ with probability $(1+g z_j+b)/2$; a reference read of a known input $\pm1$ returns $+1$ with probability $(1\pm g+b)/2$.

\paragraph{Estimates and order of operations.} Let $Q(z)=\operatorname{round}(512\,\operatorname{clip}(z,-1,1))/512$. Start-up: $\hat g_i=Q((p-m)/2048)$, $\hat b_i=Q((p+m)/2048-1)$, where $p,m$ count $+1$ outcomes among 2048 reads of inputs $+1$ and $-1$. In each epoch both observers read the world and the reference inputs on the same world state, before any exchange. Observer $i$ reads $512-2n_r$ world variables and takes the mean reading $o_i$ ($\pm1$ coded). It then updates its calibration from this epoch's references with $n_r$ reads per sign, $\hat g_i\leftarrow Q(0.75\,\hat g_i+0.25\,(p-m)/n_r)$ and $\hat b_i\leftarrow Q(0.75\,\hat b_i+0.25\,((p+m)/n_r-1))$, where $p,m$ count $+1$ outcomes among the $n_r$ reads of each known input, and only then forms $\hat x_i=Q((o_i-\hat b_i)/\max(\hat g_i,0.30))$. The report is $\hat c_i=\operatorname{sign}\hat x_i$ with sign $+$ at zero, and the scores use it. After one synchronous exchange of the values just formed, the coupling step below updates the calibration used in the next epoch.

\paragraph{Coupling.} Agreement: with innovation $e_i=o_i-(\hat g_i\hat x_j+\hat b_i)$, $\hat b_i\leftarrow Q(\hat b_i+0.25\,e_i)$, using the partner's value of the same epoch. Weighted agreement multiplies the step by $\operatorname{clip}(\hat g_j/0.4,0,1)^2$ with the partner's reported $\hat g_j$ (a negative reported gain gives weight $0$). Leader scheme: observer~1 calibrates with 64 references per sign and ignores observer~2; observer~2 applies the agreement step to observer~1's $\hat x$. Reference-only schemes exchange nothing.

\paragraph{Faults and scoring.} A fault window lasts 100 epochs and starts at an epoch drawn uniformly from $[800,2400)$ for each trajectory; the same start is used in the faulted, matched no-fault and isolation runs. A bias fault adds a nominal $0.30$ to the damaged instrument's bias before the legal clipping; a zero-gain fault sets its gain to $0$. The isolation run is identical to the faulted run except that the receiver skips its agreement update during the window. Runs last 3000 epochs and scores exclude the first 200. The world and each observer draw from separate random streams, so a fault does not change the draws of anyone else; in the no-control world a scheme without coupling therefore gives exactly the same result for the undamaged observer with and without the fault. The scorer, and only the scorer, uses $c_k$.
